How to repurpose a newsletter with AI without losing your voice

7 October 2026 · By Kelly Hartley · Wise Women Use AI

You finish a useful newsletter, send it, then open LinkedIn and start looking for a different idea.

Before you do, go back to the newsletter. You may already have the explanation, example or practical steps your next post needs.

Repurposing means adapting that thinking for another place. AI can help with the drafting. You still decide which parts are worth sharing, whether they sound like you and what the reader should do next.

Choose a piece with enough in it

Look for an article, newsletter or recording that answers a real question. It should contain a clear point and something that helps explain it: a specific example, a reason or a process.

A short announcement may only need to be an announcement. There’s no prize for turning two sentences into a twelve-slide carousel.

For this example, imagine a newsletter explaining how to reuse useful content instead of starting again for every channel. This is an illustrative example, not a customer result.

Its main point is that the same explanation can help people in different ways. Its practical step is to open an existing piece and mark the advice, example and next action.

Give each version a different job

You don’t need every format. Choose the ones your audience uses and that the original piece can support.

Four ways to reuse the useful parts of one newsletter
FormatWhat to take from the newsletterWhat the reader gets
LinkedIn postThe problem of starting from scratch after sending a useful newsletterA reason to look at work they already have
CarouselThe steps: choose a source, find its useful parts, adapt and checkA process they can save and try
Short videoOne clear answer, explained aloudA useful point they can understand quickly
Website articleThe reasoning, a worked example and the checking processEnough detail to apply the method

That is a content waterfall: one substantial piece supplying several useful versions. It should reduce repeated thinking, not leave you with a bigger pile of drafts to manage.

Show AI what should stay yours

Give it the original piece and one or two examples of your own writing that you still like. Explain who you’re speaking to and the job of the new version.

You can keep a short note alongside those examples: the words you use, the phrases you would never say and the details that must stay accurate. Add your existing colours, fonts and image choices when the output needs a visual.

This is like briefing someone helping in your business. “Make something good” leaves plenty to guess. A clear job and an example of good work give them somewhere to start.

Try this prompt with a piece you have permission to use, with private customer information removed:

Use the original piece and writing examples below. Suggest three useful ways to adapt it for [my chosen channels]. For each, name the question it answers, the part of my source it uses and what the reader can understand or do afterwards. Don’t draft yet. Don’t invent experiences, customer stories or results. Flag any missing information. Keep my meaning and qualifications.

Choose the suggestions that earn their place. Then ask for one draft at a time, with a clear format and length.

Check what disappeared in the rewrite

An AI draft can read smoothly while saying less.

Here’s an illustrative wording comparison:

Original: “Open one newsletter. Underline the advice, the example and the next step.”

Weaker rewrite: “Make the most of your existing content.”

The second version has lost the actual instruction. Your reader now has to work out what to do.

Put the draft beside the source. Check the advice, the example, the qualifications and the invitation. Read it aloud. If the useful detail disappeared, put it back.

Prepare the next step too

An approved post still needs somewhere to go and a working link if you’re inviting people to act. Save the finished copy with its image, destination and publishing date.

Keep related pieces together so you can see repetition before your audience does. If two posts offer the same thing in almost the same words, keep the stronger one or give the second a different purpose.

Start with one piece this week. Find the parts worth reusing, choose a useful new format and check that it still says what you meant.
