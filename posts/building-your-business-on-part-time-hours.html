Building your business on part-time hours

3 August 2026 · By Kelly Hartley · Wise Women Use AI

For years I planned my weeks around hours I didn't have.

Not on purpose. I'd sit down on a Sunday, look at everything the business needed, and spread it across a week that existed only on paper. Thirty-five hours, give or take. Then Monday would turn up with a full-time job in it, and a young family, and by Wednesday I was already behind on a plan that was never possible to begin with.

I did that for years, across a few different businesses. Dropshipping, e-commerce, a stint at digital marketing. The ideas were decent. What ran out every time was hours, and money I didn't have to buy my way past the shortage.

For a long while I thought that made me the problem.

There's a particular kind of tiredness that comes with that. Not the tiredness of a big week, which at least has something to show for it. The other sort, where you've worked every spare hour you had and still ended up further behind than when you started. I had a business in the UK that closed during the financial crisis. I started again in Australia. In between the proper jobs there were the side businesses, always squeezed into evenings, always running on whatever was left of me after everyone else had been seen to.

And the thing that finished off every single one of them was the same. Not the idea. Not the effort. I was working incredibly hard inside a business nobody could see, with no money for advertising and no hours left to fix that myself. I could picture exactly what I wanted to build. Getting there was always where it fell apart.

It took me longer than I'd like to admit to see that the plan was the problem. I was measuring myself against a working week that belonged to someone with nothing else pulling at them, then deciding I wasn't disciplined enough when I couldn't match it. Most business advice is written for that person. Forty hours, an empty diary, nobody else's appointments to book, no ageing parent to worry about, and a body that never once decides today is not the day.

If you've been quietly carrying that same maths, I'd like you to put it down.

Not the hours you wish you had. The hours you actually have, week in and week out, without wrecking yourself to get them.

For some women that's fifteen. For others it's twenty-five. Both can build something real. The damage comes from planning for thirty-five and then taking it personally when the week doesn't cooperate.

I know how uncomfortable that number is to write down. Mine felt like an admission. It reads like the reason you haven't got further, right up until you start planning around it, and then it becomes the most useful thing on the page.

Once the number is honest, the sorting gets easier. Not painless, but easier.

The work only you can do goes first. Client work. The conversations where someone decides whether to buy from you. The content that puts your face in front of people who've never heard of you. That's the irreplaceable part, and it's usually the part that gets shoved to Friday because everything else feels more urgent.

Everything else has to justify itself.

A great deal of what feels essential doesn't survive that question. The research that eats a morning. The email you rewrite four times before sending. The captions, the follow-ups, the proposals, the reporting, the small admin that has nothing to do with the thing you're actually good at. None of it needs you specifically. It just needs doing.

I won't pretend I found a system and everything fell into place. It was slower than that, and a lot less tidy.

What I can tell you is that AI is the first thing that ever closed the gap between what I could see and what I could actually build. Every business I tried before ran aground in the same place, and I've thought more than once about how differently those years might have gone if I'd had this then.

I came to it sideways, the way most people do. A repetitive job I was sick of doing. Then another one. It crept up on me that the tasks I'd been treating as the cost of running a business, the ones quietly eating the few hours I had, didn't actually require me to sit there doing them. They just required somebody, and that somebody didn't have to be me at eleven at night.

When I lost my job at the end of 2025, that was the thing I had. Not savings, not a runway, not a team. A way of working that meant a small number of hours could carry a whole business, and a strong sense that the women I knew were nowhere near using any of this.

I'm not telling you that from the far side of some finish line, either. I run Wise Women Use AI on exactly this. The newsletter you're reading was drafted by a setup I built, and I read it back on my walk before I touched a word of it. The posts go out while I'm doing something else entirely. I'm not saying that to show off. It's the only reason a business this size fits inside a life that already had plenty in it.

What that hands back isn't really hours on a spreadsheet. It's the headspace to stop holding all of it at once.

Three or four hours where you're genuinely delivering for someone who's paying you. An hour on content, less if you batch it. Then the admin, the calls, the back-and-forth that never entirely stops, which will take another hour and always feels like it took three. And one proper sit-down with the bigger picture. That last one is the first to go when the week gets tight, and it's the one that actually decides where you end up.

That's a real business week. Most of it needs you. The rest is already handled, or could be by the weekend.

When the hours come back, they're yours.

I won't tell you what to do with them. Some of the women I work with want to slow the whole thing down. Protect the weekend. Take the dog out before the laptop opens. Spend more of the week on the part of the business they enjoy and less on the part they endure. Others want room to think properly for the first time in years, and to grow the thing they've built.

Neither of those is the better answer. Having the choice at all is the answer, and it's the thing most of us haven't had for a very long time.

The business world will keep telling you that success takes total availability. You don't have to believe it. The women building the steadiest businesses I know aren't the ones working the most hours. They're the ones who know precisely which hours matter and guard them.

So if you're working with a smaller window than the advice assumes, you're not behind. You're doing it with better information than I had.
