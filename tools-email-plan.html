Plan and draft your next month of emails

Use your own ideas and writing samples to prepare useful emails for your list, with clear checks before anything is sent.

Claude or ChatGPT · No inbox connection needed · Work through one batch at your own pace

Bring what you already have

You sit down to email your list and end up deciding everything at once: what to say, how to start and whether to mention your offer. Separate those decisions from the writing. Plan the useful emails first, then draft them from material that is actually yours.

  • Three to five emails or pieces of writing that sound like you. Use only your own words, with private customer details removed.
  • Your audience, what they need help with and your current offer, if you want to mention one.
  • A few real ideas, customer questions or an existing article, transcript or newsletter to draw from.
  • The period you are planning and how often you realistically want to send.

Samples can come from Kit, Outlook, Gmail, a document or an earlier newsletter. Copy selected text into an ordinary Claude or ChatGPT chat. You do not need to connect your inbox or install a skill. If you have no previous emails, use a short explanation you have written or spoken yourself.

Show it how you sound

Run this once, then check the notes against your samples. Keep the useful notes in a document you can paste into future chats. If you already have a writing guide, use that instead of making another.

Writing style notes
Help me write emails to my list using my own writing as a reference.

Audience: [who reads my emails and what they need]
Samples of MY writing: [paste three to five useful examples, labelled Sample 1, Sample 2 and so on]
Words or habits I want to keep: [your notes]
Words or habits I want to avoid: [your notes]

Make a short writing reference from these samples. Cover the way I open, explain an idea, use examples, make an invitation and finish. Quote a short line from each relevant sample so I can see where your observations came from. Separate a consistent pattern from something that appears only once. Do not treat somebody else's quoted words or a standard footer as my writing style.

Ask me about anything that conflicts. Give me notes I can edit and save. Do not promise to remember them in a new chat. Do not turn sample stories, prices or old offers into current facts. Wait for me to check these notes before drafting emails.

Give each email a reason to exist

A quiet month may need two useful emails. A workshop month may need more. Let the real message decide the number, rather than filling a calendar for the sake of it.

Email plan
Help me plan the emails my list needs for this period. Do not draft them yet.

My business: [what I do]
My readers: [who they are and what matters to them now]
Period and preferred frequency: [dates and realistic sending rhythm]
Useful source material: [paste or attach ideas, articles, questions, transcripts or notes]
Current offer, if relevant: [what it helps with, inclusions, confirmed price and link, or no offer]
Real dates or deadlines: [confirmed dates only, or none]
What readers have already received: [brief notes, if known]

Suggest a small plan. For each email show: its purpose, one main idea, the source that supports it, why the reader would care, one next step and a suggested sending date. A useful email may teach, explain, answer a question or make a relevant invitation. Do not force all of these into the plan.

Do not invent scarcity, customer stories, results, events or deadlines. If the source material cannot support the number of emails I suggested, propose fewer or ask for a specific missing example. Avoid repeating the same message in slightly different words. Flag any facts or links I need to confirm. Wait for me to choose the plan.

Draft one, then the rest

Check the first email before asking for the rest. That gives the next drafts something better to follow than your original instructions alone.

First email draft
Use my checked writing reference and the email plan in this chat. Draft email [number].

Use only the source material I supplied for this email. Keep one main point and one useful next step. Aim for [my preferred length, for example 200–350 words], but do not pad a short idea.

Return two clear subject line options, one preview line and the full email. Make the opening specific to the reader's situation. Use my natural wording and spelling. Do not invent first-person experiences, testimonials, figures, dates, prices or links. If a missing fact matters, ask me before drafting rather than filling it in.

After the email, list what I need to check. This is a draft, not permission to send or schedule anything. Wait for my edits before drafting the rest.
Remaining drafts
Here is my corrected first email and what I changed:
[Paste the checked email and explain what sounded wrong or was missing.]

Apply those corrections to the writing reference. Show the changes briefly, then draft the remaining emails in the agreed plan. Give each a subject line, preview line, body and one next step. Preserve the separate purpose of each email and remove repeated explanations. Use only the facts and source material supplied. Ask about missing information. Keep every email labelled as a draft for my review.

See the difference

Worked example · fictional business

Input: A garden designer has a note about observing where afternoon shade falls before choosing plants. Her words are: “Start with what the garden gives you. The plant list comes later.” Her readers are planning small courtyard gardens. There is no promotion this week.

Useful plan: One email asking readers to notice a sunny and a shaded spot in their courtyard. Explain how those observations inform a plant shortlist. Leave watering and pot selection for later emails.

Possible opening: “Start with what the garden gives you. The plant list comes later. Before you choose plants for the courtyard, take a look at where the afternoon shade falls. That gives you something useful to bring to your planning.”

The draft uses the supplied idea and wording. It should not invent a customer garden, a plant recommendation or a promised result.

Check, save and prepare to send

  • Read each email aloud. Change anything you would not say. Check that it earns the reader’s time.
  • Verify every claim, date, offer detail and link. Fill or remove all placeholders before scheduling.
  • Paste the checked copy into the email platform you already use. Check the subject, preview text, formatting, intended audience and unsubscribe footer. Send yourself a test.
  • Choose the real sending dates there. This guide does not connect to your email platform or schedule anything.
  • Save the plan, source material and corrected writing notes together. Next time, reuse the method and update the facts.

You are finished when: each chosen email has a checked draft, a working next step and a place in your sending plan. Record the time spent, including editing. After sending, look at useful replies and relevant clicks before deciding what to repeat.
