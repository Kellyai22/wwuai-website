Where to start

Your jobs, biggest first

These are in the order I'd take them on, which is the order of how much of each one a setup can genuinely hold. Do the first one this week and leave the rest alone. Three at once is how this stops happening.

Every prompt below works in Claude or ChatGPT. Paste it in exactly as it is and answer the questions it asks you.

Answering the same question for the ninth time

You have explained what's included, how it works and what happens next more times than you could count, and you write it fresh every single time. This is the clearest win of the lot, because the answer never really changes. Only the person asking does.

The fix is one document that holds your real answers in your own words. Then you're editing something instead of starting from nothing, which takes a tenth of the energy.

The answers document
I run my own business and I answer the same handful of questions over and over. I want to write them down properly once, in my own words, so I never start from a blank page again. Interview me. Ask me one question at a time and wait for my answer before the next one. Start by asking what I sell and who buys it. Then ask me what people want to know before they buy, what they ask once they've bought, and what they get confused about or ask twice. For each one I give you, ask me how I'd answer it if the person was standing in front of me. Don't tidy my answer up. Keep my words, my level of detail, and the bits where I say "look, honestly". When we're done, give it back to me as a list of questions with my answers underneath each one, in plain sentences, no headings in title case and no bullet points inside the answers. Then tell me which three answers I'll reuse the most, so I know which ones to keep somewhere I can find them.

Hunting for something you know you've already written

You know you wrote it. You can nearly picture it. It's in an email, or a note on your phone, or a document with a name you'll never guess again. So after ten minutes of looking, you write it a second time, which is the most expensive way to save a file.

This one is almost entirely fixable, and not by being tidier. What fixes it is one home for the things you reuse, plus a rule about where new things go, so you never have to remember a decision you made months ago.

One home, one rule
I keep rewriting things I've already written, because I can't find them. I want you to help me build one simple home for the things I reuse, and one rule about where new things go. Ask me these, one at a time. What kind of things do I keep needing again (prices, replies, captions, templates, process notes)? Where do they currently end up (email, notes app, documents, my head)? And what do I actually go looking for most often? Then give me: 1. A short list of no more than six places things can live. Six, not twenty. Name them the way I'd say them out loud, not in business language. 2. For each one, the one sentence rule for what belongs there. 3. The first three things I should move in today to make it worth having. 4. A naming pattern for files so that future me can find something by searching one word. Keep it small enough that I could set it up in half an hour. If you're about to suggest something with more than six places in it, cut it down instead.

Writing the content and the captions

The empty caption box, late in the day, when you have nothing left. That's the worst possible moment to ask yourself to be interesting, and it's almost always the moment it gets done.

The part that can come off you is the drafting, not the thinking. You still decide what's worth saying. What changes is that you say it out loud once, into your phone, and get a draft back that already sounds like you rather than a blank page and a blinking cursor.

Talk it, don't type it
I'm going to talk at you for a minute about something I want to post about. It'll be rambly and out of order, because I'm saying it rather than writing it. [Use the microphone button and just talk. Or paste a transcript of a voice note here.] Your job is to find the post that's already in there. Don't add anything I didn't say. Give me back: 1. The one line I said that lands hardest, quoted exactly as I said it. 2. A caption built around it, in my words, in the order a person would actually say it. Keep my contractions and my asides. Leave it a bit untidy rather than polished. 3. Two alternative opening lines, in case the first one doesn't feel like me. Rules: no em dashes. No sentence that says what something isn't before it says what it is. No emoji as bullet points. Don't make me sound cleverer or calmer than I sounded. If I waffled and there's no real point in there, tell me that instead of inventing one.

Following up and chasing

The quote you sent three weeks ago. The invoice that's now awkward. You know exactly how to write the follow-up, and you still haven't, because following up feels like asking twice and nobody enjoys that.

So the trick is to stop treating it as a separate job you'll get to. Write the follow-up at the same moment you send the original, while you still feel warm about it, and put a date on it. Half of this work is the drafting. The other half is you deciding to send it, and that part stays yours.

The follow-up that doesn't apologise
I need to follow up on something and I've been putting it off. Here's the situation. What I sent: [the quote, invoice, proposal or message] When I sent it: [roughly when] What I know about where they're at: [anything, or nothing] How well I know them: [barely, or we get on well] Write me three follow-ups, each no longer than four sentences. One for now. One for a fortnight after that. One that closes the loop kindly and lets them off the hook without me sounding hurt. Rules for all three: don't apologise for following up, and don't say "just checking in" or "circling back". Give them one easy thing to reply to, even if the answer is no. Sound like a person who is fine either way, because I am. Warm, short, no pressure and no guilt. Then give me the two dates I should send the second and third one, counting from today.

Email and messages

You open the inbox to answer one thing and the morning is gone. The pile isn't really the problem. The problem is that every message looks equally urgent until you've read it, so you read all of them, then answer four.

Sorting can come off you completely. Writing mostly can't, because a lot of these need your actual decision. So split the job in two: let something else tell you what's in there and what it's about, then you write the handful that need you.

Sort first, write second
Here's what's sitting in my inbox. I've pasted in the sender and subject line of each one, and for some of them a bit of the message. [Paste them in. A rough list is fine.] Sort them into four groups for me: Needs a decision from me, and only me. Needs a reply, but a short one anybody could write. Someone is waiting on something I promised. Doesn't need me at all. For the second group, draft the replies now. Two or three sentences each, warm, no filler, no "I hope this email finds you well". For the first group, tell me what the actual decision is in one line each, so I'm not re-reading the whole thing to work out what's being asked. For the third group, tell me what I promised and how overdue it is. Then finish with the one thing you'd do first if you were me, and why.

Working out what to do next

This is the one that never gets counted as work, and it's the most tiring thing on the list. You're not worn out from doing the jobs. You're worn out from carrying the list of them, and from picking, all day, which one goes next.

Something else can hold the list and tell you the order. It can't decide what matters to you, and you wouldn't want it to. What it takes away is the endless re-sorting, which is where the tiredness actually comes from.

What breaks first
Here is everything I think I'm supposed to be doing. It's business and home mixed together, because that's how it lives in my head. [List it out. Messy is fine. Half sentences are fine.] Don't sort this by urgency, because everything feels urgent and that's the problem. Sort it by consequence instead. For each thing, tell me in a few words what actually happens if I don't do it this week. Then group them: Something breaks or somebody is let down. It gets worse and more awkward the longer I leave it. It's been sitting there so long that nothing has happened, which tells us something. It's mine and I want to do it, even though nobody is waiting. Then give me three things for this week. Only three. Say which one to do first and roughly how long each will really take, not the optimistic version. Last thing: tell me which items on my list I've clearly been carrying for months without acting on, and ask me straight out whether they're actually still happening.

Admin, invoicing and scheduling

This is the one where the help is smallest. The clicking is still yours. Logging into the portal, entering the numbers, moving the appointment, none of that comes off your plate from a prompt.

What does come off is the wording you rewrite every time, and the remembering. A short list you didn't have to hold in your head, plus ready-made wording for the messages you send constantly, turns a scattered hour into a focused twenty minutes.

The weekly ten minutes
Help me build a short weekly admin pass for my business, and the wording I keep rewriting. First ask me what my business does, then ask what admin comes round every week or every month, and what I always seem to find out about too late. Then give me two things. One: a weekly checklist of no more than eight items, in the order I should do them, written as actions ("check what's unpaid", not "financial review"). Put the one that costs me money if I miss it at the top. Two: the wording for the messages I send over and over. An invoice with a due date. A polite nudge on an unpaid one. Rescheduling an appointment. Confirming a booking. Short, warm, no business-speak, and written so I only have to change a name and a date. Tell me honestly which items on that checklist a person still has to do by hand, so I'm not sitting here expecting magic.

Home and family admin

The appointments. The school emails. Working out what everyone is eating this week. It doesn't count as work, so it never appears anywhere, and it comes out of exactly the same head that's supposed to be running a business.

This is the least automatable thing on your list and anyone who tells you otherwise has never run a house. What it can do is the remembering and the drafting. The week laid out once. The reply to the school. The list you didn't have to think about at the shops.

The household pass
I'm doing one pass over the home side of my week so it stops living in my head. Here's what's coming up. Who's in the house: [who, and roughly what ages if there are kids] What's on this week: [appointments, school things, sport, visitors, anything] What always catches me out: [dinner, forms, the bins, whatever it is] Give me back: 1. The week laid out day by day, with the things that have a time on them, and one line for what needs sorting the day before. 2. Seven dinners that use overlapping ingredients, with the one shopping list that covers them. Nothing that needs more than half an hour on a weeknight. 3. Anything on my list that somebody else in the house could genuinely do, said plainly, so I can ask. Then tell me which single thing on this list, if I sorted it on Sunday, would take the most weight off the rest of the week.

One honest note

About those numbers

The hours you could get back are my estimates, not a study. They come from running this business almost entirely this way, and from watching what actually shifts for the women I work with. I've kept them deliberately conservative, and I've told you where the help is weakest rather than pretending it's even across the board.

Your own numbers will land differently, and the first week you try one of these you'll find out where yours sit. That's more useful than any figure on this page.
